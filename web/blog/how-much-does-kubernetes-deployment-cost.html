How Much Does Your Kubernetes Deployment Actually Cost?

Cost · 7 min read

Ask most teams what a given Deployment costs per month and you'll get a shrug, or a link to last month's total cloud bill with no way to break it down by workload. Kubernetes doesn't bill you directly — the cloud provider bills for the nodes underneath it — so the connection between "this YAML file" and "this many dollars a month" is one most teams never actually calculate until the bill is a surprise.

It's a calculable number, though, and you don't need a running cluster to get a reasonable estimate. Here's what actually drives Kubernetes cost, and how to estimate it from a manifest alone.

What actually costs money

A pod itself has no price. What you're paying for is the compute (and storage) it reserves on a node — and the number that matters isn't what the container actually uses at runtime, it's what it requests, because that's what the scheduler reserves and what determines how many nodes you need.

  • CPU and memory requests — resources.requests.cpu and resources.requests.memory, multiplied by replica count, is the core of the calculation.
  • Persistent storage — a PersistentVolumeClaim's requested size, priced per GB/month against the relevant storage class.
  • Replica count — a Deployment with replicas: 3 costs three times what the same pod spec costs at replicas: 1.

Why "no requests set" means "no cost estimate"

If a container doesn't set resources.requests, there's genuinely nothing to estimate from — the scheduler treats it as effectively weightless for bin-packing purposes, which in practice means it can consume node capacity unpredictably. This is one of Cubzi's own warning rules (missing resource requests/limits), and it's not a coincidence that it's also the single most common reason a cost estimate comes back at $0: the manifest simply hasn't specified what it needs.

A worked example

Take a Deployment with 3 replicas, each requesting 500m CPU and 512Mi memory, plus a 20Gi PVC on a standard storage class. On AWS, that's roughly equivalent to reserving 1.5 vCPU and 1.5Gi of memory across the fleet, priced against blended on-demand compute rates, plus the PVC's per-GB monthly storage rate. Multiply, add, and you have a monthly range — not a guaranteed bill (actual node packing, spot pricing, and discounts all shift the real number), but a useful one for comparing "should this be 3 replicas or 2" before you apply the YAML.

Why estimate before deploying, not after

Cost tools that parse Terraform or connect to a running cluster tell you what you're already paying. That's useful for a monthly review, but it's too late to change a decision that's already in production. Estimating from the manifest itself — in a PR, before merge — turns "why is our bill up 30% this month" into a number a reviewer sees before they approve the change.
