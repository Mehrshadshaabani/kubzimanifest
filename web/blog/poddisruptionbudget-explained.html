PodDisruptionBudgets Explained: Staying Available During Node Drains

Reliability · 5 min read

Most Kubernetes reliability problems people worry about are dramatic — a bad deploy, a crashed dependency, a traffic spike. PodDisruptionBudgets protect against something much more mundane: routine cluster maintenance, the kind that happens on a schedule and is supposed to be invisible to your users.

Voluntary vs involuntary disruptions

Kubernetes distinguishes between disruptions your application caused (a crash, an OOM kill — involuntary) and disruptions the cluster operator chose to trigger — draining a node for a Kubernetes version upgrade, a node pool resize, or routine host maintenance. These are called voluntary disruptions, and by default, nothing stops all of them from happening to a single Deployment's replicas at once.

What can go wrong without one

Say you run a Deployment with 3 replicas, spread (by chance or by an anti-affinity rule) across 3 nodes. A cluster upgrade drains those nodes one at a time — normally fine, since only one replica goes down at a time and the other two keep serving. But if 2 of your 3 replicas happen to land on the same node, and that node gets drained, you lose two-thirds of your capacity simultaneously, and if your remaining single replica can't absorb the full load, users see errors or timeouts during what was supposed to be a routine, invisible operation.

What a PodDisruptionBudget does

A PodDisruptionBudget (PDB) tells the cluster's eviction API: don't voluntarily evict pods from this set if doing so would violate this budget. It doesn't prevent the drain — it makes the drain wait, evicting one pod, waiting for a replacement to become ready, then continuing.

apiVersion: policy/v1
kind: PodDisruptionBudget
metadata:
  name: api-pdb
spec:
  minAvailable: 2
  selector:
    matchLabels:
      app: api

With minAvailable: 2 on a 3-replica Deployment, the eviction API will evict at most one pod at a time — enforcing that at least 2 stay available throughout the drain, regardless of how the replicas happen to be distributed across nodes.

minAvailable vs maxUnavailable

You can express the same budget either way — minAvailable: 2 and maxUnavailable: 1 mean the same thing for a 3-replica Deployment. maxUnavailable tends to be easier to reason about for Deployments that autoscale, since it stays proportionally correct as replica count changes (e.g. maxUnavailable: 25%), while minAvailable is often clearer for a fixed, small replica count where you know the exact floor you need.

Only matters above one replica

A single-replica Deployment doesn't benefit from a PDB — there's nothing to keep available during an eviction of the only replica, so this is specifically a multi-replica concern, which is why it's checked only when replica count is 2 or higher.
